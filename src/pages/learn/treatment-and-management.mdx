There is currently no cure for alpha-gal syndrome. Most healthcare providers recommend that people with AGS stop eating meat from mammals ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)), so treatment revolves around avoiding your triggers, being ready for a reaction, preventing new tick bites, and building routines that make daily management sustainable. The good news is that once you learn your specific triggers and develop routines around them, living well with AGS is entirely possible.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Dietary avoidance

The foundation of AGS management is removing mammalian products from your diet. This sounds straightforward but involves more than just skipping red meat.

**Foods to eliminate:**
- All mammalian meat: beef, pork, lamb, goat, venison, bison, rabbit ([CDC product list](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html))
- Organ meats and processed meats (sausage, hot dogs, bacon, deli meats)
- Broths and stocks made from mammalian bones or meat
- Gelatin in all forms (desserts, gummy vitamins, marshmallows, gel capsules)
- Lard, tallow, and suet used in cooking or baked goods

**Foods that require caution:**
- **Dairy** — Milk and milk products may contain alpha-gal, but CDC notes that many patients with AGS can tolerate them ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). Tolerance is individual: some people do fine with dairy, others react to butter or cream, and a few react to any trace. Work out your own limits with your allergist rather than by trial and error.
- **"Natural flavors"** — This label can hide mammalian-derived ingredients. When in doubt, contact the manufacturer to ask about the source.
- **Food cooked in mammal fat or on shared surfaces** — Some people with AGS react to products made or cooked with mammal fat ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). Shared grills, fryers, and flat-tops at restaurants or cookouts are worth asking about. See [label reading and cross-contamination](/learn/label-reading-and-cross-contamination).

**Safe food categories:**
- Poultry (chicken, turkey, duck), fish, and seafood ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html))
- All fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds
- Eggs
- Plant-based dairy alternatives (oat, almond, coconut, soy)

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Reading labels

Ingredient checking becomes a daily habit for AGS patients. U.S. law requires labels to declare nine major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies)). Mammalian meat and alpha-gal are not on that list, so you have to read the full ingredient list rather than relying on the "Contains" statement. The milk warning does help if you avoid dairy.

**Ingredients to flag:**
- Gelatin (bovine or porcine)
- Natural flavors (source often unspecified)
- Beef or pork fat, lard, tallow
- Casein, whey, lactose (dairy derivatives — relevant if you are dairy-sensitive)
- Glycerin and magnesium stearate (in medications and supplements; may be animal-sourced)
- Stearic acid (can be animal or plant-derived)

Carmine (cochineal) is made from insects, not mammals, so it is not a source of alpha-gal.

Our [ingredient scanner](/scanner) can help identify mammalian-derived ingredients in product photos or barcode scans.

**Tips from experience:**
- Do not assume a product is safe because it was safe last time. Manufacturers change formulations without notice.
- Store brands and name brands of the same product may have different ingredients.
- "Vegan" labeled products are generally safe, but verify — some products labeled "plant-based" still contain dairy-derived ingredients.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Medication safety

Managing medications with AGS requires active involvement with your pharmacist and medical team. Many common medications contain mammalian-derived inactive ingredients that are not obvious from the drug name.

**Gelatin capsules** are the most widespread concern. Many prescription and over-the-counter medications come in gel caps made from bovine or porcine gelatin. For most medications, a tablet or vegetarian capsule alternative exists. Ask your pharmacist to check and switch when possible.

**Magnesium stearate and glycerin** are common inactive ingredients that may come from mammals; CDC lists both, along with gelatin and bovine extract ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). The amounts are small and many people with AGS tolerate them, but if you are highly sensitive, ask your pharmacist to check the source.

**Heparin** deserves attention. This blood thinner is usually made from pig intestine and is used routinely in hospitals, during surgery, and in some IV lines. It is on CDC's list of medical products that may contain alpha-gal ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)), and reactions have been reported ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). Make sure it is flagged in your chart so your care team can weigh the risks and alternatives before a planned procedure. In an emergency, do not refuse treatment; tell the team about your AGS and let them decide.

**Vaccines:** Some vaccines contain gelatin as a stabilizer. CDC says reactions to vaccine and medication ingredients are very rare but can happen in people with severe AGS, and recommends talking with your provider before a new medication or vaccine ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). Many people with AGS have received gelatin-containing vaccines without problems ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). Don't skip vaccines on your own; ask your allergist whether any extra precautions make sense for you.

**Supplements:** Gummy vitamins often contain gelatin. Omega-3 fish oil capsules may use bovine gelatin for the softgel. Look for vegetarian or vegan-certified alternatives.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Emergency preparedness

Even with careful avoidance, accidental exposures happen. Being prepared for a reaction is a non-negotiable part of AGS management.

**Epinephrine:**
- Your allergist will help you decide which epinephrine device to carry ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/meat/)), and CDC recommends learning how to use it with your provider ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)).
- Keep it with you, not in a hot car or a drawer at home.
- Know your warning signs from your written action plan. Trouble breathing, throat tightness, faintness, or symptoms in more than one body system are reasons to act, not wait.
- Check the expiration date regularly and replace it before it expires.

**Emergency action plan:**
- Work with your allergist to create a written action plan that specifies when to use antihistamines versus epinephrine, and when to call 911.
- Share this plan with family members, close friends, and coworkers.
- If you use epinephrine, go to the emergency room. Symptoms can go away and then return a few hours later, so you need medical observation ([AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/anaphylaxis)).

**Antihistamines:**
- Your allergist may recommend an antihistamine for mild symptoms such as limited hives. Ask which one and what dose belong in your plan.
- Antihistamines do not stop anaphylaxis. They are not a substitute for epinephrine in a severe reaction.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Medical alerts for emergencies

Standard emergency room medications can be dangerous for AGS patients. If you arrive at an ER unconscious or unable to communicate, the medical team needs to know about your allergy before they administer anything.

- **Medical alert tattoo** — Some people with AGS choose a wrist tattoo such as "Alpha-gal allergy: no mammalian products." It cannot be lost, but responders may not look for it, so pair it with a bracelet or wallet card. See our [tattoo guide](/learn/medical-alert-tattoo) for free designs and the trade-offs.
- **Medical alert bracelet** — Engraved wristbands from providers like Road ID or MedicAlert are widely recognized by first responders.
- **Phone lock screen** — Set your lock screen to display your allergy, emergency contact, and key medication restrictions.
- **Inform local EMS** — Some 911 systems allow you to register medical conditions that dispatchers can relay to responding paramedics.
- **Hospital records** — Make sure your AGS diagnosis, and a note about mammal-derived products such as heparin, are in your records at every hospital and clinic you use.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Navigating social situations

AGS changes how you participate in shared meals, and the social dimension can be as challenging as the dietary one.

**Family gatherings and holidays:**
- Bring your own food or coordinate with the host about safe dishes. Offering to bring a main dish ensures you have something to eat and introduces others to AGS-safe cooking.
- Be direct about what you can and cannot eat. Vague explanations lead to well-meaning mistakes.
- Expect that some people will not fully understand the severity. A brief, clear explanation works better than a detailed medical lecture.

**Dining out:**
- Many people with AGS eat out less often, because sauces, cooking fats, and shared surfaces can hide mammalian ingredients.
- When you do eat out, call ahead and speak with a manager or chef. A printed [dining card](/learn/dining-card-guide) that explains your allergy is more effective than a verbal explanation in a busy kitchen.
- Seafood restaurants and fully vegan restaurants tend to be easier. Many Asian cuisines rely on fish, seafood, and poultry, but pork, beef broth, and lard are common too, so ask about each dish. (Fish sauce and oyster sauce come from seafood, not mammals.)
- Ask specifically about cooking fats (butter, lard), broth bases, and whether your food will touch a shared grill.

**Travel:**
- Research restaurants and grocery stores at your destination before you go.
- Pack safe snacks and meals for transit.
- Carry your EpiPen, antihistamines, and a printed allergy explanation card — especially when traveling where your language may not be spoken.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Working with your medical team

AGS is still unfamiliar to many healthcare providers. Being an informed and proactive patient makes a significant difference in the quality of care you receive.

- **Find an allergist who knows AGS.** CDC found that many providers are still unfamiliar with it ([MMWR, 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7230a1.htm)). It is the single most important medical relationship for someone with AGS. If your allergist has not treated alpha-gal patients before, consider finding one who has. The [AAAAI](https://allergist.aaaai.org/find/) and [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/find-an-allergist/) directories can help.
- **Educate your other providers.** Your primary care doctor, dentist, pharmacist, and any specialists should know about your diagnosis. Bring printed information if needed — many providers will encounter AGS for the first time through you.
- **Before any procedure**, confirm that the surgical and anesthesia team knows about your allergy and has flagged mammal-derived products such as heparin, gelatin-based products, and animal-tissue heart valves in your chart.
- **Prevent new tick bites.** New bites may reactivate the allergy, and people who avoid them may see their sensitivity fade over time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). See our [prevention guide](/learn/prevention).
- **Keep a copy of your blood test results** and your allergist's letter on your phone for situations where you need to quickly explain your condition to a new provider.
