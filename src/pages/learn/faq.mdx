## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> What is alpha-gal syndrome?

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy linked to tick bites, most often the lone star tick in the United States ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/about/index.html)). People with AGS react to galactose-α-1,3-galactose ("alpha-gal"), a sugar molecule made by most mammals but not by people. Eating mammalian meat or products made from mammals can then trigger reactions ranging from stomach symptoms and hives to anaphylaxis. See our full explainer on [what alpha-gal syndrome is](/learn/what-is-alpha-gal).

## <IconBrandTick className="inline-block align-text-bottom"/> How are tick bites connected to AGS?

Alpha-gal can be present in tick saliva and may enter the body during a bite ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/meat/)). In some people, the immune system responds by making IgE antibodies against alpha-gal, and from then on eating mammalian products can trigger a reaction. New tick bites may reactivate the allergy, which is why preventing them is part of managing AGS ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). See the [prevention guide](/learn/prevention).

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Why are reactions delayed?

Most food allergies cause symptoms within minutes. Alpha-gal reactions usually start **2 to 6 hours** after eating ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)). The leading explanation is that alpha-gal in meat is attached to fats, which are absorbed slowly through the lymphatic system and peak in the blood about four hours after a meal ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). The delay is one of the main reasons AGS goes undiagnosed: few people connect late-night symptoms to dinner.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> What foods do I need to avoid?

The primary triggers are meats from mammals: beef, pork, lamb, goat, venison, bison, and rabbit, plus organ meats ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). Alpha-gal is also in:

- **Gelatin** made from beef or pork, found in gummy vitamins, gel capsules, marshmallows, and gelatin desserts.
- **Mammal fats** such as lard, tallow, and suet, and foods cooked in them.
- **Broths and gravies** made from beef or pork, including bouillon and stock used as a base in soups, sauces, and restaurant dishes.
- **Dairy**, for some people (see the next question).

Poultry, fish, seafood, eggs, fruits, and vegetables do not contain alpha-gal.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Can I eat dairy?

Maybe. Milk and milk products may contain alpha-gal, but CDC notes that many patients with AGS can tolerate them ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). Some people eat dairy freely; others react to high-fat dairy like cream, butter, or ice cream; and a few react to small amounts. There is no reliable way to predict your tolerance in advance. Work it out with your allergist rather than by trial and error.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Are medications safe?

Most medications are fine, but some contain inactive ingredients that may come from mammals. CDC lists gelatin, glycerin, magnesium stearate, and bovine extract ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). Things to know:

- **Gelatin capsules** — ask your pharmacist whether a tablet or vegetarian capsule is available.
- **Glycerin and magnesium stearate** — often plant-sourced, but your pharmacist can check with the manufacturer if you are highly sensitive.
- **Heparin**, pig or cow heart valves, some monoclonal antibodies, and certain antivenoms may contain alpha-gal. Make sure your diagnosis is in your medical records so your care team can weigh the risks and alternatives.
- **Vaccines** — some contain gelatin. Reactions are very rare, and many people with AGS have received gelatin-containing vaccines without problems ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). CDC recommends talking with your provider before a new vaccine or medication ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)).

See our [treatment and management guide](/learn/treatment-and-management) for more on medication safety and emergency preparedness.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Will AGS go away?

Sometimes. People with AGS who avoid new tick bites often see their alpha-gal IgE levels fall over time; people with ongoing bites generally do not ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). CDC says some people who avoid additional bites may be able to tolerate alpha-gal products again, and that this is an individualized decision to make with your provider ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). There is no approved cure or desensitization treatment yet; see the [research tracker](/research) for studies in progress.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> How do I get tested?

Diagnosis combines your history, an exam, and a blood test for IgE specific to alpha-gal ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)). Common lab orders:

- **Labcorp** — test [650001](https://www.labcorp.com/tests/650001/galactose-%CE%B1-1-3-galactose-alpha-gal-ige), Galactose-α-1,3-galactose (Alpha-Gal) IgE
- **Quest Diagnostics** — test 10554 (Alpha-Gal IgE) or 10555 (Alpha-Gal Panel, which adds beef, pork, and lamb)

A positive test alone does not mean you have AGS; many people have alpha-gal IgE without symptoms. The result has to fit your history of delayed reactions. Visit our [testing page](/testing) for lab links, locations, and direct-to-consumer options.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Can I eat at restaurants?

Yes, with planning. Hidden mammalian ingredients usually show up in sauces, broths, cooking fats, and shared grills and fryers, so many people with AGS eat out less often or stick to a few places they trust.

Strategies that help:
- Seafood restaurants and fully vegan restaurants tend to be easier.
- Call ahead and ask to speak with a manager or chef. A [dining card](/learn/dining-card-guide) explains AGS faster than a conversation in a busy kitchen.
- Ask specifically about butter or lard, broth bases, and shared grills and fryers.
- Expect that some kitchens cannot guarantee a mammal-free meal, and have a backup plan.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> What should I tell the ER?

If you go to the ER for any reason, tell the team right away:

- You have alpha-gal syndrome, an allergy to mammal-derived products.
- Some medical products, such as heparin, may contain alpha-gal ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)); please check before giving them.
- Whether you have used epinephrine, and when.

If a reaction is severe, get emergency care immediately ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)), and do not refuse treatment while you wait for answers about ingredients. Because you may not be able to speak for yourself, carry a [wallet card](/learn/wallet-card-guide) and consider a medical ID bracelet or a [medical alert tattoo](/learn/medical-alert-tattoo).

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Where can I find support?

Other people with AGS have already worked out which brands are safe, which doctors understand the condition, and how to handle daily life. Places to connect:

- **Facebook groups** — several large, active AGS support groups share product alerts, recipes, and doctor recommendations.
- **Reddit** — the r/alphagal subreddit is another active community.
- **Your allergist** — the most important medical partner you have. The [AAAAI](https://allergist.aaaai.org/find/) and [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/find-an-allergist/) directories can help you find one.

Community advice is a great place to find ideas, but check anything medical with your allergist.
