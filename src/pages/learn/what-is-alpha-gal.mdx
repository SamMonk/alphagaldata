Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy linked to tick bites. People with AGS react to a sugar molecule called galactose-α-1,3-galactose, or "alpha-gal," which is made in the bodies of most mammals but not in people ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/about/index.html)). That is why beef, pork, lamb, venison, and other mammal products can cause reactions. Unlike typical food allergies, reactions usually start **2 to 6 hours** after eating ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)), which makes the condition notoriously hard to connect to a meal.

## How alpha-gal syndrome develops

In the United States, AGS is most often associated with bites from the lone star tick (*Amblyomma americanum*). A few cases have followed bites from blacklegged and western blacklegged ticks ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/about/index.html)), and researchers have linked other tick species to the allergy in Europe, Australia, and Asia ([NIAID workshop report, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)).

Alpha-gal can be present in tick saliva and may enter the body during a bite ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/meat/)). In some people, the immune system responds by making immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies against alpha-gal. Once someone is sensitized, eating mammalian meat or another alpha-gal product can set off an allergic reaction. The allergy can take weeks or months to develop after a bite ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/meat/)).

This is unusual because most food allergies are reactions to proteins. Alpha-gal is a carbohydrate, which is one reason the condition was not described until the early 2000s ([AGA, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36958889/)) and why it does not behave like a typical food allergy.

## Why reactions are delayed

With most food allergies, symptoms appear within minutes. The leading explanation for the delay in AGS involves fat digestion. In meat, alpha-gal is attached to fats (glycolipids), and dietary fat is absorbed slowly: it is packaged into particles called chylomicrons that travel through the lymphatic system before reaching the bloodstream, peaking about four hours after a meal. Lab studies have shown beef alpha-gal glycolipids being packaged this way ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). That timing lines up with the hours-long delay people with AGS experience.

## What triggers reactions

The most obvious triggers are meats from mammals: beef, pork, lamb, venison, rabbit, and organ meats such as liver ([CDC product list](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). Poultry, fish, seafood, eggs, fruits, and vegetables do not contain alpha-gal. Less obvious sources include:

- **Dairy.** Milk and milk products may contain alpha-gal, but CDC notes that many patients with AGS can tolerate them. Sensitivity varies widely from person to person, so decide about dairy with your allergist.
- **Gelatin** made from beef or pork, found in gummy candy, marshmallows, and gel capsules.
- **Mammal fats and broths:** lard, tallow, suet, and meat broth, bouillon, stock, and gravy.
- **Medications and vaccines.** CDC lists gelatin, glycerin, magnesium stearate, and bovine extract as ingredients that may come from mammals. Your pharmacist can usually find out the source.
- **Medical products.** Heart valves from pigs or cows, heparin (a blood thinner), some monoclonal antibodies (such as the cancer drug cetuximab), and certain antivenoms may contain alpha-gal ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). This is why medical alert identification matters in an emergency.

People with AGS might not react to every alpha-gal product, and reactions can differ from one exposure to the next, even with the same food ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)). That inconsistency is real, not imagined, and it is one more reason the condition is easy to miss.

## How common is AGS?

CDC identified more than 110,000 suspected cases of AGS between 2010 and 2022, and estimates that as many as 450,000 people may be affected ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/about/index.html)). The range in CDC's analysis runs from about 96,000 to 450,000, because many people are never tested ([MMWR, 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7230a2.htm)). Suspected cases cluster in southern, midwestern, and mid-Atlantic states where lone star ticks live. See how that has spread on our [tick and awareness map](/heat-map).

Awareness is a big part of the problem. In a 2022 CDC survey, 42% of health care providers had never heard of AGS, and another 35% were not confident diagnosing or managing it ([MMWR, 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7230a1.htm)).

## How AGS differs from other food allergies

| Feature | Typical food allergy | Alpha-gal syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger molecule | Protein | Carbohydrate (sugar) |
| Reaction timing | Usually minutes | Usually 2–6 hours |
| How it starts | Varies; often begins in childhood | Linked to tick bites, often in adults |
| Main test | Skin prick or blood test for the food protein | Blood test for alpha-gal IgE, plus history |
| Can it fade? | Some childhood allergies are outgrown | IgE often declines if you avoid new tick bites |

## Can AGS go away?

Sometimes. People with AGS who avoid new tick bites often see their alpha-gal IgE levels fall over time, while those who keep getting bitten generally do not ([NIAID workshop report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32057766/)). CDC says some people who avoid additional tick bites may be able to tolerate alpha-gal products again, and that this is an individualized medical decision ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)). Do not test this on your own; reintroducing foods should be planned with an allergist. New tick bites may reactivate the allergy, so tick prevention is part of treatment ([prevention guide](/learn/prevention)). There is no approved cure or desensitization therapy for AGS today.

## Next steps

If you suspect AGS, look for an allergist who is familiar with it. Diagnosis is based on your history, an exam, and a blood test for alpha-gal-specific IgE. A positive test alone does not mean you have AGS, so the test has to be read alongside your symptoms ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html)). Our [testing guide](/testing) lists lab test codes and how to find a knowledgeable provider.
