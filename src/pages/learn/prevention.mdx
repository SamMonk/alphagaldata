There is currently no vaccine or medication that prevents alpha-gal syndrome. The only known way to reduce your risk is to avoid tick bites — especially from the lone star tick, the primary vector for AGS in the United States. For people who already have AGS, preventing additional tick bites is just as important: new bites may reactivate allergic reactions, while people who avoid them may see their sensitivity fade over time ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/managing/index.html)).

## <IconBrandTick className="inline-block align-text-bottom"/> Personal tick protection

**Repellents that work:**
- **Permethrin-treated clothing.** CDC recommends treating clothing and gear (boots, pants, socks, tents) with products containing 0.5% permethrin, which stay protective through several washings ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html)). You can also buy pre-treated clothing. Do not apply permethrin to skin.
- **EPA-registered skin repellents** containing DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), para-menthane-diol (PMD), or 2-undecanone ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html)). Follow the label directions, including how often to reapply. EPA's online search tool can help you choose one.

**Clothing choices:**
- Wear long pants tucked into socks and long-sleeved shirts when spending time in wooded, brushy, or grassy areas.
- Light-colored clothing makes ticks easier to spot before they reach skin.
- Closed-toe shoes rather than sandals — ticks are most commonly picked up at ankle height.

**Tick checks:**
- Check your clothing for ticks before you come inside.
- Do a full-body tick check with a mirror or a helper. Pay special attention to the armpits, in and around the ears, the belly button, the backs of the knees, the hair, between the legs, and around the waist ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html)). Lone star tick nymphs are small and easy to miss.
- Shower within two hours of coming indoors. CDC notes this has been shown to reduce the risk of Lyme disease, and it is a good moment to check for ticks.

**After outdoor activity:**
- Tumble dry clothes on high heat for 10 minutes to kill ticks on dry clothing ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html)). If clothes need washing first, dry them afterward on high heat.
- Check gear, backpacks, and any items that were on the ground.

## <IconBrandTick className="inline-block align-text-bottom"/> Yard and property management

Your yard is where most residential tick exposure happens. Reducing tick habitat around your home makes a meaningful difference:

- **Keep grass mowed** and remove leaf litter, tall grass, and brush, especially where your lawn meets woods or fields ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/prevention/index.html)).
- **Create a barrier.** CDC suggests a 3-foot-wide strip of wood chips or gravel between lawns and wooded areas.
- **Let in sun.** Trim tree branches and shrubs, and keep play equipment, decks, and patios away from grassy, brushy, or wooded areas.
- **Manage wildlife access.** Deer are a major host for adult lone star ticks. Fencing, deer-resistant plants, or removing attractants (bird feeders near the house, unsecured garbage) can reduce deer traffic through your property.
- **Consider professional tick treatment.** A licensed pest-control professional can treat yard edges and wooded borders. Ask what product they use and how long to keep children and pets off treated areas.

## <IconBrandTick className="inline-block align-text-bottom"/> Protecting pets

Pets that spend time outdoors can carry ticks directly into your home and onto furniture and bedding:

- Use veterinarian-recommended tick prevention year-round. Oral preventatives (like NexGard or Bravecto) and topical treatments (like Frontline) are the most common options. Talk to your vet about what works best for your area.
- Check pets for ticks after they come inside, especially around ears, between toes, and under collars.
- Keep pet bedding clean and inspect it regularly.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> Understanding your risk area

CDC describes the lone star tick as a very aggressive biter that is widely distributed in the Northeast, South, and Midwest ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/about/where-ticks-live.html)), and suspected AGS cases cluster in the same regions ([MMWR, 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7230a2.htm)). Ticks are most active in the warmer months, but they can be out on mild days in any season, especially in the South.

## <IconInfo className="text-teal-700"/> If you find an attached tick

Remove the tick as soon as possible with clean, fine-tipped tweezers ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/after-a-tick-bite/index.html)):

1. Grasp the tick as close to the skin's surface as possible.
2. Pull it away from the skin with steady, even pressure. Don't twist or jerk it.
3. Clean the bite area and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol, or hand sanitizer.
4. Never crush a tick with your fingers. Seal it in a bag or container, or wrap it in tape. Keeping it lets your doctor see what kind of tick it was.
5. Check the rest of your body; there may be more than one.

Do not use nail polish, petroleum jelly, or heat. CDC warns these can agitate the tick and force fluid from it into your skin. The goal is to get the tick off quickly, not to wait for it to let go.

AGS can take weeks or months to develop after a bite ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/meat/)). If you start getting delayed hives, stomach symptoms, or swelling after eating mammalian foods, see an allergist and ask about an alpha-gal IgE blood test ([testing guide](/testing)). If you get a rash or fever in the weeks after a tick bite, see your doctor too; lone star ticks also spread other illnesses such as ehrlichiosis.
