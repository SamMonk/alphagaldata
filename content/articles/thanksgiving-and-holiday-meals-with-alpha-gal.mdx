The turkey is rarely the problem. With alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), the risk at a holiday meal hides in the **gravy, stuffing, sides, and desserts**, and in a kitchen that has handled bacon and sausage all day. Here is how to plan the meal, whether you are hosting, going as a guest, or bringing a dish.

<AtAGlance>
  <Fact label="Turkey itself is not a trigger">Alpha-gal is not in poultry, fish, or eggs ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)).</Fact>
  <Fact label="The risk is in the extras">Butter, milk, lard, beef stock, bacon, sausage, and gelatin show up in sides and desserts.</Fact>
  <Fact label="Reactions come late">Usually 2 to 6 hours after eating, so an afternoon bite can cause symptoms that evening ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)).</Fact>
  <Fact label="Dairy is personal">Many people with AGS tolerate milk products and some do not. Know your own line before the meal.</Fact>
</AtAGlance>

<Callout tone="emergency" title="If a reaction is severe">
  Use prescribed epinephrine and call 911, even if it starts hours after the meal. This guide is educational, not medical advice; your allergist decides what you personally need to avoid.
</Callout>

## What alpha-gal is actually in

Alpha-gal is a sugar found in meat and other products from mammals ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/about/index.html)). CDC's list of [products that may contain alpha-gal](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html) groups the food sources like this:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Mammal meat and organs">Beef, pork, lamb, venison, rabbit, and others. **Not** poultry, fish, shellfish, or eggs.</Card>
  <Card title="Animal fats and extracts">Lard, tallow, suet, beef broth or stock, bouillon, gelatin, and meat gravy.</Card>
  <Card title="Dairy (for some people)">Milk is a major allergen, so U.S. packaged foods must list it in plain words on the label ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies)).</Card>
  <Card title="Some medicines">Gelatin, glycerin, magnesium stearate, and bovine extract can come from mammals. Worth checking on holiday cold and flu remedies too.</Card>
</CardGrid>

People with AGS don't react to every alpha-gal product, and the same food can cause a different reaction from one time to the next ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)). For the full ingredient list, see [Alpha-Gal 101](/learn/alpha-gal-101) and [label reading and cross-contamination](/learn/label-reading-and-cross-contamination).

## The holiday plate, item by item

A guide to the usual U.S. Thanksgiving table. "Usually fine" assumes the dish was cooked plainly and kept away from the others.

<PlateGuide>
  <PlateGroup level="fine">
    <PlateItem name="Roast turkey or chicken">Poultry, so no alpha-gal. Watch for butter rubs, bacon on top, and [carrageenan](#carrageenan-check-the-turkey-label) in the added solution.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Roasted vegetables">When tossed in olive oil, not butter or bacon fat.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Homemade cranberry sauce">Fruit, sugar, and water.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Green salad">With a vinaigrette rather than a creamy dressing.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Rice or wild rice">Cooked in water or poultry broth.</PlateItem>
  </PlateGroup>
  <PlateGroup level="ask">
    <PlateItem name="Gravy">Often made with butter and milk. Gravy mixes and restaurant gravies can contain beef stock, beef fat, or gelatin.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Mashed potatoes">Butter, cream, and milk are standard.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Green bean casserole">Condensed cream soups usually contain dairy, and toppings can contain fat from mammals.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Dinner rolls">May contain milk solids, whey, or lard.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Pumpkin pie">Fillings often use evaporated or condensed milk. Crusts can use lard or butter.</PlateItem>
  </PlateGroup>
  <PlateGroup level="avoid">
    <PlateItem name="Sausage or bacon stuffing">Pork. Boxed stuffing mixes can also contain beef fat or beef flavor.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Bacon-topped sides">Brussels sprouts, green beans, or salads with bacon or pancetta.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Marshmallows and gummies">Usually made with mammal gelatin ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)).</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Charcuterie board">Prosciutto, salami, and other cured pork.</PlateItem>
    <PlateItem name="Ham">Pork, the centerpiece at many winter holidays.</PlateItem>
  </PlateGroup>
</PlateGuide>

<Callout tone="tip" title="The small additions catch people out">
  A splash of cream in the squash soup, sausage crumbles in the cornbread, bacon fat in the cast-iron pan. When in doubt, ask "what's in it *and* what was it cooked in?"
</Callout>

## Carrageenan: check the turkey label

<Callout tone="info" title="Why this section is here">
  Shawn, who runs this site and lives with AGS, reacts to carrageenan, and it can be in the turkey itself.
</Callout>

Carrageenan is a thickener and stabilizer extracted from red seaweed. It is widely used in processed foods ([Borsani et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684400/)). It doesn't come from a mammal, but it has a connection to alpha-gal:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="A similar sugar link">Carrageenan is built from galactose units, some joined by the same alpha-1,3 galactose link that defines alpha-gal ([Borsani et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684400/); [McGuire et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33127644/)).</Card>
  <Card title="Reported by about 1 in 9">In a 2026 survey of 562 people with AGS who contacted a medical information service, 10.85% reported a carrageenan allergy, more than any food except dairy ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)). Both were self-reported, and the authors estimate that 1–2% of AGS patients react, so ask your allergist whether you should avoid it.</Card>
  <Card title="Why it's in turkey">USDA allows carrageenan "to extend and stabilize" poultry products, and in breading mixes and sauces ([9 CFR 424.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-424.21)). The same rule lets cured pork use it to keep injected brine from leaking out, which is the moisture-holding job it does in injected meats.</Card>
  <Card title="More in our full guide">The research, other foods, and the medications that can contain it are in [carrageenan and alpha-gal](/learn/carrageenan-and-alpha-gal).</Card>
</CardGrid>

The good news is that the label has to tell you. Raw turkey with an added solution must include the percentage of solution and every ingredient in that solution in the product name ([9 CFR 381.117](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-381.117)).

<Steps>
  <Step title="Look at the product name on the front">Phrases like "contains 15% added solution of…" mean something was injected or added.</Step>
  <Step title="Read every ingredient in the solution">Look for the word "carrageenan." The solution's ingredients have to be listed with that statement.</Step>
  <Step title="When in doubt, buy plain">A turkey with no added solution is just turkey. A fresh, plain bird or breast lets you season it yourself.</Step>
  <Step title="Check the extras that come with it">If the turkey comes with a gravy packet, read that label too. Check ready-made gravies, sauces, and stuffing mixes for carrageenan as well as for mammal ingredients.</Step>
</Steps>

## Cross-contact in a busy kitchen

A dish can have no mammal ingredients and still pick some up along the way. FDA requires food factories to have written plans against allergen "cross-contact" because tiny amounts can move from one surface to another ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies)). A home kitchen on Thanksgiving has the same risks on a smaller scale:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Shared tools">The tongs that turned the bacon go into the green beans.</Card>
  <Card title="Cutting boards">Raw sausage, a quick wipe, then the vegetables.</Card>
  <Card title="Seasoned pans">A cast-iron skillet seasoned with bacon fat, used for cornbread.</Card>
  <Card title="Buffet lines and butter dishes">Spoons migrate between dishes and crumbs end up in the butter.</Card>
</CardGrid>

More on this in [label reading and cross-contamination](/learn/label-reading-and-cross-contamination).

## If you are hosting

The goal: your guest with AGS shouldn't have to question every dish.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Plan the menu with them">Ask, in writing if you can, what they avoid and what's fine. Don't assume dairy is a problem, and don't assume it's safe because they ate it last year.</Step>
  <Step title="Build around a safe main">Roast turkey, chicken, or fish seasoned with olive oil, salt, pepper, and herbs instead of butter or bacon fat.</Step>
  <Step title="Make a few sides without mammal ingredients">Olive-oil roasted vegetables, plain rice, soup made with poultry broth or water, a vinaigrette salad, and homemade cranberry sauce.</Step>
  <Step title="Cook the safe dishes first">Before the bacon, sausage, or beef broth comes out. Give them their own clean tongs and spoons and a separate spot on the counter.</Step>
  <Step title="Keep the labels">Save the turkey wrapper and the boxes for broth, stuffing mix, rolls, pie crust, and marshmallows. The "Contains:" line shows milk; the full ingredient list is where lard, beef fat, gelatin, and "natural flavors" show up ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies)).</Step>
  <Step title="Plate their meal before the buffet opens">Handing them a plate from the kitchen avoids the swapped-spoon problem entirely.</Step>
  <Step title="Know where the epinephrine is">Ask where their auto-injector will be during the meal and who else knows how to use it.</Step>
</Steps>

## If you are the guest

You can be gracious and still protect yourself.

<Timeline>
  <When label="A week before" title="Send the host a short note">
    "Here's what I avoid, here's what I can eat, and here's what I'll bring. Happy to answer questions." Lead with what you *can* have.
  </When>
  <When label="The day before" title="Cook a full plate you know is safe">
    A main, a side, and a dessert you can actually eat. If nothing else on the table works for you, you still have a meal.
  </When>
  <When label="When you arrive" title="Ask about the big four">
    **Turkey:** any added solution or carrageenan? **Gravy:** what stock and what fat? **Stuffing:** any sausage or bacon, and what broth? **Dessert:** what's in the crust, the filling, and any whipped topping or marshmallows?
  </When>
  <When label="At the table" title="Keep epinephrine on you">
    In your pocket or bag, not in a coat in another room. Tell at least one other adult where it is and how to use it.
  </When>
  <When label="That evening" title="Remember the delay">
    Reactions usually start 2 to 6 hours after eating ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/signs-symptoms/index.html)). Know your plan before you head home or settle in for the night.
  </When>
</Timeline>

## What to bring

Dishes that travel well, don't need much counter space, and are naturally free of mammal meat and fat:

<Checklist title="Safe-to-arrive-with dishes (tick off what you're making)">
  <Check>Herb-roasted turkey breast: olive oil, salt, pepper, sage, thyme</Check>
  <Check>Roasted carrots, parsnips, and sweet potatoes in olive oil</Check>
  <Check>Wild rice and mushroom pilaf made with water or poultry broth</Check>
  <Check>Green salad with lemon and olive oil vinaigrette</Check>
  <Check>Cranberry-orange sauce from scratch</Check>
  <Check>Fruit crumble with an oat and olive oil topping, or a sorbet</Check>
</Checklist>

<Callout tone="warning" title="“Non-dairy” doesn’t mean animal-free">
  Check the labels of packaged broths, baking mixes, margarines, and baking spreads for lard, tallow, or beef. Our [label reading guide](/learn/label-reading-and-cross-contamination) shows how to scan a label quickly.
</Callout>

## Questions for your allergist before the holidays

<Checklist>
  <Check>Which foods should I keep avoiding, and is there anything (like dairy) I could try again? Many people with AGS tolerate milk products, but not all ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/alpha-gal-syndrome/data-research/products-containing-alpha-gal/index.html)).</Check>
  <Check>Should I avoid carrageenan, and what about other additives that aren't from mammals?</Check>
  <Check>How many epinephrine pens should I carry while traveling, and are mine in date?</Check>
  <Check>Could alcohol or exercise around a meal change how I react?</Check>
  <Check>Do any of my seasonal medicines (cold and flu remedies, for example) contain gelatin or other mammal-derived ingredients?</Check>
  <Check>What should I do if a reaction starts hours after a holiday meal, after your office has closed?</Check>
</Checklist>

Alpha-gal doesn't have to shrink the holidays. With a menu conversation, a safe main, a few known-good sides, and a kitchen that knows about cross-contact, most families can work out a Thanksgiving that's comfortable for everyone at the table.
