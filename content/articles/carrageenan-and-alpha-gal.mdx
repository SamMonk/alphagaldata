Carrageenan is a seaweed thickener, not a mammal product, so it isn't on the standard alpha-gal avoid lists. But it can carry the same sugar that causes alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), and in one 2026 survey it was the most commonly reported food allergy among people with AGS after dairy ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)). Here is what is known, what isn't, and how to find it on labels if you react to it.

<AtAGlance>
  <Fact label="Not from a mammal">A thickener and stabilizer extracted from red seaweed, used widely in processed foods ([Borsani et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684400/)).</Fact>
  <Fact label="Can carry alpha-gal">Carrageenan "can also contain the alpha-gal epitope," the part of the sugar the allergy reacts to ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)).</Fact>
  <Fact label="About 1 in 9 in one survey">10.85% of 562 people with AGS who contacted a medical information service said they were allergic to carrageenan.</Fact>
  <Fact label="Not everyone reacts">The same authors put the share of AGS patients who react at 1–2% and say more research is needed.</Fact>
</AtAGlance>

<Callout tone="info" title="Why we wrote this">
  Shawn, who runs this site and lives with AGS, reacts to carrageenan. If you do too, you are not imagining it, but you may also be in a minority. Your allergist can help you work out whether to avoid it.
</Callout>

<CardGrid>
  <Card badge="Survey · conference abstract, 2026" title="Carrageenan reported more than most major allergens">
    Researchers reviewed two months of questions sent to Pill Clarity, a free medical information center, by people who said they had AGS. Carrageenan was reported more often than tree nuts, shellfish, soy, eggs, peanuts, or wheat. The authors call it "a frequently reported allergen in AGS patients" and say the mechanism still needs to be worked out ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)).
  </Card>
  <Card badge="Lab study, 2024" title="Carrageenan fragments bound anti-alpha-gal antibodies">
    Small pieces of carrageenan, made in the lab, bound human antibodies that recognize alpha-gal ([Sokolova et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37985065/)). This shows a chemical overlap. It doesn't show what happens when a person with AGS eats carrageenan.
  </Card>
  <Card badge="Chemistry" title="A similar sugar link">
    Carrageenan is built from galactose units, some joined by the same alpha-1,3 link found in alpha-gal ([Borsani et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684400/); [McGuire et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33127644/)).
  </Card>
  <Card badge="Letter, 2015" title="The question was raised a decade ago">
    A letter in the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology* pointed to carrageenan and the alpha-gal epitope in 2015 ([Tobacman, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26518095/)). Few studies of people with AGS have followed.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Callout tone="warning" title="Keep in mind">
  The survey is a short conference abstract, not a full peer-reviewed paper. Both the AGS diagnosis and the carrageenan allergy were self-reported, not confirmed by testing. People who contact a medication information service may worry about additives more than most, so the 10.85% probably doesn't describe everyone with AGS. The first author works at Pill Clarity, whose inquiries were studied.
</Callout>

## Where carrageenan shows up

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Food and drinks">Some brands of chocolate milk and tofu ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)), and processed foods generally, where it is used as a thickener, gelling agent, emulsifier, or stabilizer ([Borsani et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684400/)).</Card>
  <Card title="Poultry, sauces, and breading">USDA allows it "to extend and stabilize" poultry products, and in breading mixes and meat sauces ([9 CFR 424.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-424.21)). That includes some turkeys sold with an added solution.</Card>
  <Card title="Medications">Some manufacturers' versions of acetaminophen, omeprazole, and dabigatran etexilate capsules contain it ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)). The same medicine from a different manufacturer may not, so check the exact product.</Card>
  <Card title="Holiday meals">Turkey labels, gravy packets, and ready-made sauces are the usual places to look. See our [Thanksgiving guide](/learn/thanksgiving-and-holiday-meals-with-alpha-gal#carrageenan-check-the-turkey-label).</Card>
</CardGrid>

## How to check for it

<Steps>
  <Step title="Food: read the ingredient list">Carrageenan is listed by name. It isn't one of the nine major food allergens ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)), so it won't appear on a "Contains:" line, which covers only those ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies)). You have to read the full list.</Step>
  <Step title="Turkey and other raw meat: read the product name">Raw meat and poultry with an added solution must state the percentage of solution and every ingredient in it as part of the product name ([9 CFR 381.117](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-381.117)). Look there for carrageenan.</Step>
  <Step title="Medications: ask your pharmacist">Ask them to check the inactive ingredients for the exact manufacturer you were given, and to note carrageenan alongside your alpha-gal allergy in your pharmacy record. The study authors suggest that people who react to alpha-gal in medications be asked specifically about carrageenan ([Uchi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2025.12.676)).</Step>
  <Step title="Recheck when products change">Brands and generic manufacturers change formulas. A product that was carrageenan-free last year may not be now.</Step>
</Steps>

Our recipes already flag carrageenan where an ingredient often contains it, such as plant milks and broth. See [recipes](/recipes) and [label reading and cross-contamination](/learn/label-reading-and-cross-contamination).
